3-  topologies

**Bus topology**  
All devices connect to a single shared backbone cable, with terminators at both ends. Every device sees all traffic on the cable, so devices share the same collision domain and use CSMA/CD to manage access. It’s cheap and simple, but one cable break takes down the whole network, and performance drops as devices are added. It’s obsolete today and mostly relevant for historical context (Ethernet 10BASE2/10BASE5).

**Star topology**  
Every device connects to a central device, usually a switch (formerly a hub), with its own dedicated cable. This is the most common LAN topology today. A single cable failure affects only one device, and adding or troubleshooting devices is easy. The downside is that the central switch is a single point of failure, and it needs more cabling than a bus.

**Full mesh topology**  
Every device connects directly to every other device. The number of links is n(n-1)/2, so 5 devices need 10 links and 10 devices need 45. It offers the highest redundancy and reliability, since traffic can take many paths if a link fails. But it’s expensive and complex because of the links and interfaces required, so it’s used only in small, critical environments (such as core or WAN links between a few important sites).

**Partial mesh topology**  
Some, but not all, devices have direct links to each other. Critical nodes are typically connected to several others, while less important nodes have fewer connections. It gives much of the redundancy of a full mesh at a lower cost, so it’s common in WAN designs and in campus and data center networks. It’s less fault-tolerant than a full mesh, and some paths may be longer.
